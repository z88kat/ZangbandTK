M	how-they-fight
T	h2	How they fight
T	h2	\n
T	p0	Angband’s monster AI has exactly one goal in it. Every road through it leads to the player: following the sound and scent that spread out from wherever you’re standing, or running away from the same place, or working with its pack to surround you. None of that describes an ally, so allies think differently.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	They pick a fight. An ally looks for something it’s at war with, within about twenty squares, that it can reach, and goes for it. Once it has picked something it stays with it until the fight is over or the target stops being worth chasing. It will not drop a wounded enemy because something fresher walked into the room.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	They stay awake. Angband decides whether a monster is thinking by asking whether it can see, hear or smell 
T	p0 em	you
T	p0	. An ally on the far side of a level does none of those, and would otherwise sleep through its own battle.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	They cast at what they’re fighting. A pet with spells throws them at its enemy, rolling against that creature’s armour instead of your saving throw.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	They will not hit you, and they will not interrupt you. An ally that steps into the square you’re standing in is trying to get past you, not attacking you, so it goes around instead, and waits if there’s nowhere to go around to. Nor does its shuffling about count as something moving in view: you can rest with a pet standing over you, which you couldn’t do if the game treated your own animal as a warning. Only a creature that has turned hostile does either.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	The noise it makes elsewhere doesn’t interrupt you either. A pet bursting through a door still announces itself (you hear a door burst open), but hearing it doesn’t stop you resting, and nor does a fight you can’t make out. An ally trading spells with something out of your sight, or beyond it, is left to get on with it; a fight where you can see one of the two stops you, as it should. Anything aimed at 
T	p0 em	you
T	p0	 always stops you, seen or unseen.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	And when two enemies meet, they fight. Neither shoves past the other, and neither tramples the other underfoot, so a large hostile monster can’t walk over your pet and delete it on the way to you. It has to kill it, one blow at a time, where you can see it happening.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	A pet fighting for you earns you nothing. Experience is for the killing blow, and if your pet struck it, the blow wasn’t yours. A pet cannot finish a unique either: uniques survive anything that isn’t the player at one hit point.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	Pets also keep to a leash: how close they stay, whether they’ll fight near you, whether they open doors, whether they pick things up. Those are orders you give, and the next section covers giving them. Until you do, every pet follows the default, which is to stay within about six squares, leave doors alone and leave your things where they fall.
T	p0	\n

T	h3	\n

M	losing-one
T	h2	Losing one
T	h2	\n
T	p0	Pets are easily irritable. Anything you do that hurts one turns it against you: a blow, an arrow, a bolt that passes through it, a fireball that catches it at the edge. It stops being yours on the spot, and it remembers.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	That’s the thing to think about before lighting up a room while you have pet orcs, and it’s why area spells are a different proposition with a stable behind you. Damage from 
T	p0 em	other
T	p0	 monsters does not count against you: a pet caught in something else’s breath is unhurt in its opinion of you.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	Carrying something that aggravates turns them all. Nobody wants to be your friend if you’re aggravating.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	Turning on a creature that trusted you costs you virtue: a gain in Individualism and a loss in Honour, Justice and Compassion. Those are real numbers here, and other things read them.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	Making one costs virtue too, in the opposite direction. Every successful charm takes a point off your Individualism, since taking a creature into your service is a step away from standing alone, and if what you charmed was an animal it adds a point to Nature. A character who builds a menagerie of beasts will find both scales have moved a long way without a single message saying so. See 
L	p0 link	3	virtues	Virtues
T	p0	.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	Walking into your own pet does 
T	p0 em	not
T	p0	 hurt it. You change places with it (“you push past it”), and that’s what stops the whole mechanism firing every time an animal gets into a doorway ahead of you.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	Unless you aren’t in command of yourself. If you’re confused, hallucinating, stunned, berserk, or unable to see what’s in front of you, the blow lands and the pet takes it personally. Confusion is a different kind of dangerous when you have things following you.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	Changing a pet’s shape doesn’t change its side. Polymorph one with Chaos’s 
T	p0 em	Polymorph Other
T	p0	, or a Wand or a Rod of Polymorph, and whatever it turns into is still yours.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	Polymorphing your own animal deep in a dungeon is how a weak pet becomes a strong one, because the new shape is drawn from somewhere between the creature’s own depth and the depth you’re standing on. It’s where the dragons come from. It’s a gamble every time: you don’t get to choose what you get, it can come back worse, and a unique won’t change at all. What holds it in check is the upkeep. A stronger pet is a heavier one, and the charge is on the 
T	p0 em	sum
T	p0	 of your pets’ levels.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	It never takes the creature away. The shape drawn is always one that can stand where your pet is standing, so a polymorph on the shore won’t turn your hound into a kraken and leave you with an empty patch of sand.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	Chaos itself isn’t so obliging. A chaos attack that polymorphs also 
T	p0 em	hurts
T	p0	, and hurting a pet turns it against you before its shape has changed, so a chaos ball dropped on your own animal costs you the animal, whatever it becomes.
