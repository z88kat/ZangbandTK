T	h2	New objects
T	h2	\n
T	p0	There are eighty-six of Zangband’s own object kinds, which is most of what it had that Angband does not: thirteen swords including the Diamond Edge and the Psiblade, nine polearms, eight hafted weapons, eighteen pieces of armour from the T-shirt to the feathered Hagaromo, and twenty-five rings and amulets. The remaining thirteen are consumables: five potions, four scrolls, two rods, a food and an arrow.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	The jewellery is where the interesting things are. An Amulet of Anti-Magic stops you casting at all, and an Amulet of Anti-Teleportation roots you where you stand, which is a drawback until the day something tries to teleport you away. A Ring of Lordly Protection is what its name suggests. An Amulet of Destruction is not.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	A Ring of Wizardry gives a caster more mana. The number on it is per level you can cast at, so it is worth a little to a novice and a great deal to someone who has been at it a while. The Ring of Fate has a section of its own, below.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	Among the consumables, a Rod of Havoc picks one of twenty-five damage types and throws it at whatever you aimed at, as a ball or occasionally as a beam, and is never twice the same rod. A Scroll of Mundanity strips an item back to the plain thing it was made as: no artifact, no ego, base dice, base armour, and the identification forgotten with them. It is the only way to be rid of something that will not come off, and it’s indiscriminate. Nothing warns you what you’re about to lose. (A magical figurine keeps its monster, which is what it was made as.)
T	p0	\n
T	p0	Zangband renamed a good deal of what it inherited from Angband. Its Ring of Skill is Angband’s Ring of Accuracy, and its Scroll of Enchant Weapon Deadliness is Enchant Weapon To-Dam, so importing those by name would have put two of each in the game under two names. They are compared by the slot they occupy instead of by what they’re called, so the ten renamed ones are recognised as things Angband already has.
T	p0	\n
M	the-ring-of-fate
T	h2	The Ring of Fate
T	h2	\n
T	p0	It sounds like a lucky charm, and it isn’t, quite. It sharpens every critical hit in the game by half again, including the ones landed on you. And roughly one monster in thirteen generated while you wear it comes from deeper than the level should produce, sometimes forty levels deeper.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	So it is a good ring for a character who kills things quickly and a very bad one for a character who doesn’t, and it will eventually introduce you to something you were not ready for. Zangband’s own source comments the third of its four effects with “Luck isn’t always good for you…”, which is the whole item in six words.
T	p0	\n
M	the-ancient-and-foul-curse
T	h2	The Ancient and Foul Curse
T	h2	\n
T	p0	Some equipment has the Curse of Topi Ylinen, and you’ll know it: the item describes itself as ancient and foul.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	Roughly once every hundred turns, a cursed item stirs and one misfortune befalls you: monsters woken, something summoned, experience drained, paralysis, a statistic lost, or your memory torn away. But each of those has a chance of 
T	p0 em	falling through
T	p0	 to the next, and the next after that, so a woken monster can escalate into a summoning, into paralysis, into total amnesia, in a single visitation. When it finishes, there’s a one-in-three chance it begins again. Sometimes the curse stirs and nothing happens at all.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	Free action does not make you immune to the paralysis. It gives you a saving throw, and shortens the paralysis if you fail.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	Cursed equipment is only worth carrying if what it gives you makes up for all this, and often it does.
T	p0	\n
M	activations
T	h2	Activations
T	h2	\n
T	p0	Zangband scripted its artifact activations in a scripting language ZangbandTK doesn’t have, so each one uses the nearest Angband activation instead. An artifact that threw a fireball still throws a fireball, with the same recharge time, but the effect is Angband’s version and won’t behave exactly like Zangband’s.

T	h2	The lotus
T	h2	\n
T	p0	There’s a mushroom that takes your memory. It looks like every other mushroom you haven’t identified, some colour and a comma on the floor, and eating it says only that you feel a little dizzy. Five turns later you forget everything you know:
T	p0	\n
T	li1	•\t
T	li1	the map of the level you are standing on;
T	li1	\n
T	li1	•\t
T	li1	the world map, and every town and dungeon mouth you had found on it;
T	li1	\n
T	li1	•\t
T	li1	everything you had learned about every monster;
T	li1	\n
T	li1	•\t
T	li1	what every flavoured thing is, so your potions go back to being coloured liquids and have to be drunk to find out what they are;
T	li1	\n
T	li1	•\t
T	li1	and every spell you had learned, which must be studied again from the book.
T	li1	\n
T	p0	It takes nothing you cannot get back: no experience, no levels, no items. But none of it comes back quickly, so the price of eating a strange mushroom is measured in hours of play. A consumable that could end a run outright is one nobody eats twice.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	One thing survives: you always know where home is. The village you started in stays on the map, along with the country immediately around it, so there’s always one place you can name and one direction worth walking. The reason is the magetower, which only carries you to places you’ve found. A character who had forgotten every place including their own would have a blank map, no travel and nothing to aim at.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	That’s also where the idea comes from. The first of Zelazny’s Amber novels opens on a man who has lost his memory and knows only that there is a place called Amber, and that he is of it.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	Something that took your memory the moment you swallowed it would be an ordinary bad mushroom. The five turns are long enough to work out what you’ve done, and not long enough to do anything about it.
