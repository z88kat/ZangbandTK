T	h2	Your home village
T	h2	\n
T	p0	The village you start in is the one exception (WLD-12). Its shops are fixed: a general store, an alchemist, a bookseller and your home. T	h2	\n
T	p0	There’s one house, and every town’s front door opens onto it. Every town in the world keeps a home, whatever else it does or doesn’t have, and they’re all the same house. Leave a sword in your village, walk four days to a great city, open the door there, and the sword is on the shelf.

M	work
T	h2	Work
T	h2	\n
T	p0	Some buildings hand out work. Walk into an inn and the innkeeper asks what you’re after: work, or a bed for the night. Ask about work and you may be offered a job: kill so many of a creature, come back, and be paid.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	Quest-giving is a property a building has, not a building of its own. There’s no “quest giver’s hut” in the game; the inn just has the property at the moment, and it could as easily be the magetower commissioning a retrieval. That way new sources of work cost a line rather than a new building.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	The inn has it because it’s where people who have been somewhere else are sitting. And since a town that has fallen keeps no services at all, the work dries up exactly where you’d expect it to, without any rule needing to say so. Every inn is hiring; a village has no inn, and nor does a town that has fallen.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	You can carry eight jobs at once, and a ninth is refused. Any building that hires will tell you how far along you are, or pay you when you’re done: twenty gold for each creature asked for, multiplied by how deep that creature lives, and never less than twenty in total. See 
L	p0 link	3	quests	Quests
T	p0	 for the rest of the terms.

T	h2	What you dream at the inn
T	h2	\n
T	p0	Some nights you dream, and what you dream depends on where you’re sleeping.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	A true dream shows you a place. The nearest town or dungeon mouth you haven’t found yet appears on the world map, and you wake knowing its name and where it stands. That’s the reason to pay for a bed on a night you could have walked through.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	For a town, that’s all it does. The magetower only carries you to towns you’ve stood in, and a dream isn’t a visit, so you still have to make the walk, but now you know which way to walk, which is the hard part. A dungeon mouth is different. The mages only need to have seen one, and a dreamed one has been seen, so it goes straight onto their list.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	A dark dream sets something hunting you. It’ll be something you’ve met: whatever you’ve seen and remember, leaning towards the worst of it. If you hold your nerve you merely remember the thing in the morning. If you don’t, you wake frightened or confused, and it wears off. A character who has met nothing memorable yet has nothing to dream about.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	Which you get depends on the law of the town. A lawful city is a good place to sleep and a frontier town isn’t:

T	tr0 odd	A well-ordered city                     about 1 night in 4   almost never\n
T	tbllast0	\n

T	h2	Walls and gates
T	h2	\n
T	p0	Every place is walled, with gates no more than two grids wide. A gate has a door on each grid; you open it to pass through, and it swings shut behind you after a while. Monsters don’t walk in through an open gate as a matter of course, but a gate you leave standing open is a gate.
T	p0	\n
M	roads
T	h2	Roads
T	h2	\n
T	p0	The towns are joined by roads, and following one is how you find the next place.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	A road takes the cheapest way across the country, so it runs down the valleys, keeps out of the swamp and goes round a mountain instead of over it. It keeps to the land, too. If two towns end up on either side of an inland sea a causeway is built, but that’s the exception. Roads don’t take short cuts across bays.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	Every town and every dungeon is on the network. The roads are laid as a spanning tree over the towns first, so there’s always a road out of your home village and following it reaches every other town. Any two towns within thirty blocks of each other then get a road directly between them, which turns the tree into a network in settled country. Finally every dungeon mouth gets a spur to the nearest town.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	Measured before the spurs existed, six of the thirteen dungeon mouths happened to sit on a road and the rest were between eleven and sixty-two blocks from one: up to a thousand grids of open country to search with nothing to follow. Better siting can’t fix that, because a dungeon stands in the kind of country it belongs in, and the deep ones belong a long way from any town. So the road goes to them instead.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	A road is two grids wide. One grid is too few: where a road turns a right angle in the block you’re standing in, a single-grid corner is one square of floor at right angles to the way you’re going, and the road reads as though it stopped. The first report of this was a road that “appears to end at the beach” after a long walk, when in fact it had turned. Two grids makes a corner a two-by-two block of paving, which reads as a turn. Three was tried first and looked like a motorway.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	About three blocks in a hundred have a road through them.
T	p0	\n
T	p0	If you’re lost, find a road and walk along it. It goes somewhere: a town, or a way down.
T	p0	\n
